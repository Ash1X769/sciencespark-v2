## SECTION 3.1 — Conservation of Mass

---

**C3-01** | Topic: Conservation of Mass | Tier: F

Q: What is the **law of conservation of mass**? *(2 facts)*

A:
- No atoms are **created or destroyed** in a chemical reaction
- So the **total mass of reactants** = the **total mass of products**

Hint: Mass in = mass out. Atoms just rearrange into new substances.

Exam Q: "State the law of conservation of mass." [1 mark]
→ Mass of reactants equals mass of products / no atoms are created or destroyed.

Notes link: /notes/c3-conservation-of-mass

---

**C3-02** | Topic: Conservation of Mass | Tier: F

Q: Give **2 reasons** why the mass of a reaction mixture might appear to **decrease**.

A:
- A **gas is produced** as a product
- The gas **escapes** into the atmosphere — reducing the measured mass of the container

Hint: Gas escapes → container gets lighter. But total mass is still conserved.

Exam Q: "A student reacts calcium carbonate with acid in an open flask. The mass decreases. Why?" [2 marks]
→ CO₂ gas is produced (1) + gas escapes from the flask (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-conservation-of-mass

---

**C3-03** | Topic: Conservation of Mass | Tier: F

Q: Give **2 reasons** why the mass of a reaction mixture might appear to **increase**.

A:
- A **gas is absorbed** from the atmosphere during the reaction
- The extra mass of the absorbed gas **adds to** the mass of the product

Hint: Magnesium burning in air — absorbs oxygen → product is heavier than the original metal.

Exam Q: "A student burns magnesium ribbon in air. The mass of the product is greater than the original magnesium. Why?" [2 marks]
→ Magnesium reacts with oxygen from the air (1) + oxygen is absorbed / adds mass to the product (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-conservation-of-mass

## SECTION 3.2 — Relative Formula Mass

---

**C3-04** | Topic: Relative Formula Mass | Tier: F

Q: What is the **relative formula mass (Mr)**? *(2 facts)*

A:
- The **sum** of the relative atomic masses (Ar) of all the atoms in a formula
- Found using the **periodic table**

Hint: Add up all the Ar values. Don't forget to multiply by subscript numbers.

Exam Q: "Calculate the relative formula mass of water (H₂O). [Ar: H = 1, O = 16]" [1 mark]
→ (2 × 1) + 16 = 18.

Notes link: /notes/c3-relative-formula-mass

---

**C3-05** | Topic: Relative Formula Mass | Tier: F

Q: Calculate the **Mr** of each compound. *(4 facts)*

A:
- **H₂O** = (2 × 1) + 16 = **18**
- **CO₂** = 12 + (2 × 16) = **44**
- **NaCl** = 23 + 35.5 = **58.5**
- **CaCO₃** = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = **100**

Hint: Write out each element, multiply by its subscript, then add. Use the periodic table for Ar values.

Exam Q: "Calculate the Mr of CaCO₃. [Ar: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16]" [1 mark]
→ 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100.

Notes link: /notes/c3-relative-formula-mass

## SECTION 3.3 — The Mole and Moles Calculations

---

**C3-06** | Topic: The Mole | Tier: F

Q: What is a **mole**? *(2 facts)*

A:
- A unit for measuring the **amount of substance**
- One mole contains **6.02 × 10²³** particles (Avogadro's number)

Hint: Like a "dozen" means 12, a "mole" means 6.02 × 10²³ particles.

Exam Q: "What is Avogadro's number?" [1 mark]
→ 6.02 × 10²³.

Notes link: /notes/c3-mole

---

**C3-07** | Topic: The Mole | Tier: F

Q: What is the **formula** for calculating moles from mass? *(2 facts)*

A:
- **Moles = mass (g) ÷ Mr**
- Rearranges to: **mass = moles × Mr**

Hint: n = m/Mr. Always check units — mass must be in grams.

Exam Q: "Calculate the number of moles in 44 g of CO₂. [Mr of CO₂ = 44]" [2 marks]
→ Moles = 44 ÷ 44 = 1 mole. Show working for both marks.

Notes link: /notes/c3-mole

---

**C3-08** | Topic: The Mole | Tier: H

Q: Give **3 steps** for calculating the **number of particles** in a sample.

A:
- Calculate moles: **moles = mass ÷ Mr**
- Multiply by Avogadro's number: **particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³**
- Give answer in correct **standard form** and **units**

Hint: Moles first, then × 6.02 × 10²³. Answer will be a very large number.

Exam Q: "Calculate the number of carbon atoms in 2380 g of carbon. [Ar of C = 12]" [2 marks] *(AQA June 2024, Q08)*
→ Moles = 2380 ÷ 12 = ~198.3 (1) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.19 × 10²⁶ atoms (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-mole

## SECTION 3.4 — Balancing Equations

---

**C3-09** | Topic: Balancing Equations | Tier: F

Q: Give **3 rules** for **balancing chemical equations**.

A:
- The number of each type of **atom** must be equal on both sides
- Only change the **numbers in front** of formulas — never change the formulas themselves
- Check **every element** has been balanced before finishing

Hint: Count atoms on each side. Add big numbers in front — never change the small numbers inside the formula.

Exam Q: "Balance this equation: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O" [1 mark]
→ 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

Notes link: /notes/c3-balancing-equations

---

**C3-10** | Topic: Balancing Equations | Tier: F

Q: What does a **balanced symbol equation** tell us? *(3 facts)*

A:
- The **reactants** (left of arrow) and **products** (right of arrow)
- The **formula** of each substance
- The **ratio of moles** — the numbers in front show how many moles react

Hint: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O means 2 moles of H₂ react with 1 mole of O₂ to make 2 moles of H₂O.

Exam Q: "In the equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, what does the 2 in front of H₂ mean?" [1 mark]
→ 2 moles of hydrogen react.

Notes link: /notes/c3-balancing-equations

---

**C3-11** | Topic: Balancing Equations | Tier: H

Q: Give **3 steps** for calculating **reacting masses** from a balanced equation.

A:
- Write the **balanced equation**
- Work out the **moles** of the substance you know: moles = mass ÷ Mr
- Use the **mole ratio** from the equation to find moles of the other substance, then calculate its mass

Hint: Balanced equation → moles of known → mole ratio → moles of unknown → mass.

Exam Q: "How much water is produced when 4 g of hydrogen reacts with excess oxygen? 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O [Mr: H₂ = 2, H₂O = 18]" [3 marks]
→ Moles H₂ = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 mol (1) → mole ratio 1:1 so 2 mol H₂O (1) → mass = 2 × 18 = 36 g (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-balancing-equations

## SECTION 3.5 — Limiting Reactants

---

**C3-12** | Topic: Limiting Reactants | Tier: H

Q: What is a **limiting reactant**? *(2 facts)*

A:
- The reactant that is **completely used up** first in a reaction
- It **limits** how much product can be made

Hint: Runs out first = controls how much product forms. The other reactant is in excess.

Exam Q: "What is meant by a limiting reactant?" [2 marks]
→ The reactant used up first (1) + it limits the amount of product formed (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-limiting-reactants

---

**C3-13** | Topic: Limiting Reactants | Tier: H

Q: What does it mean when a reactant is in **excess**? *(2 facts)*

A:
- There is **more than enough** of this reactant for the reaction
- Some of it is **left over** after the reaction is complete

Hint: Excess = leftover. Used to make sure the other reactant fully reacts.

Exam Q: "A student added excess zinc to copper sulfate solution. Why was excess zinc used?" [1 mark] *(AQA June 2024, Q02)*
→ To ensure all the copper sulfate reacts / is completely used up.

Notes link: /notes/c3-limiting-reactants

## SECTION 3.6 — Percentage Yield and Atom Economy

---

**C3-14** | Topic: Percentage Yield | Tier: H

Q: What is the **percentage yield** formula? *(2 facts)*

A:
- **% yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100**
- Always less than **100%** in practice

Hint: What you got ÷ what you should have got × 100. The theoretical yield is calculated from the equation.

Exam Q: "A reaction should produce 10 g of product but only 7 g is obtained. Calculate the % yield." [2 marks]
→ (7 ÷ 10) × 100 = **70%**. Show working for both marks.

Notes link: /notes/c3-yield

---

**C3-15** | Topic: Percentage Yield | Tier: H

Q: Give **3 reasons** why percentage yield is always **less than 100%**.

A:
- The reaction may be **reversible** — products turn back into reactants
- **Side reactions** produce unwanted products instead
- Some product is **lost** during separation, filtration or transfer

Hint: Reversible reactions, side reactions, practical losses. Never 100% in reality.

Exam Q: "Give two reasons why the percentage yield is less than 100%." [2 marks]
→ Any 2 from: reversible reaction / side reactions / product lost during collection / incomplete reaction.

Notes link: /notes/c3-yield

---

**C3-16** | Topic: Atom Economy | Tier: H

Q: What is **atom economy**? *(3 facts)*

A:
- A measure of how much of the **reactants** end up as the **desired product**
- **% atom economy = (Mr of desired product ÷ sum of Mr of ALL products) × 100**
- High atom economy means **less waste** — better for the environment and more cost-effective

Hint: How efficiently atoms are used. 100% = all atoms end up in the product you want.

Exam Q: "Why is a high atom economy important for industrial reactions?" [2 marks]
→ Less waste produced (1) + more sustainable / cheaper / better for environment (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-yield

## SECTION 3.7 — Concentration of Solutions

---

**C3-17** | Topic: Concentration | Tier: H

Q: What is the **formula** for concentration of a solution? *(2 facts)*

A:
- **Concentration (mol/dm³) = moles ÷ volume (dm³)**
- Or: **concentration (g/dm³) = mass (g) ÷ volume (dm³)**

Hint: c = n/V. Volume must be in dm³ (litres). 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.

Exam Q: "Calculate the concentration of a solution containing 2 moles of NaCl in 500 cm³ of solution." [2 marks]
→ Volume = 500 ÷ 1000 = 0.5 dm³ (1). Concentration = 2 ÷ 0.5 = **4 mol/dm³** (1).

Notes link: /notes/c3-concentration

---

**C3-18** | Topic: Concentration | Tier: H

Q: Give **3 steps** for converting between **cm³ and dm³**.

A:
- 1 dm³ = **1000 cm³** — they are the same as litres and millilitres
- cm³ → dm³: **divide by 1000**
- dm³ → cm³: **multiply by 1000**

Hint: cm³ ÷ 1000 = dm³. Always convert before using the concentration formula.

Exam Q: "A solution has a volume of 250 cm³. Give this volume in dm³." [1 mark]
→ 250 ÷ 1000 = **0.25 dm³**.

Notes link: /notes/c3-concentration

## SECTION 3.8 — Required Practical

---

**C3-19** | Topic: Required Practical | Tier: F

Q: Give **4 steps** for making a **salt by reacting an acid with an insoluble solid** (e.g. copper sulfate from copper oxide and sulfuric acid).

A:
- Add **excess solid** to the acid — stir and warm gently until no more dissolves
- **Filter** to remove excess solid
- **Evaporate** some water from the solution to concentrate it
- Leave to **crystallise** — filter and dry the crystals

Hint: Excess solid → filter → evaporate → crystallise → dry.

Exam Q: "Describe how to make pure crystals of copper sulfate from copper oxide and sulfuric acid." [4 marks]
→ One mark per bullet above.

Notes link: /notes/c3-required-practical

---

**C3-20** | Topic: Required Practical | Tier: H

Q: Give **4 steps** for a **titration** to find the volume of acid needed to neutralise an alkali.

A:
- Add the **alkali** to a conical flask with a few drops of **indicator**
- Fill a **burette** with the acid
- Add the acid **slowly** from the burette — swirling the flask
- Record the volume of acid added when the **indicator changes colour** (the end point)

Hint: Alkali in flask + indicator → acid from burette → stop at colour change → read volume.

Exam Q: "Describe how to carry out a titration to find the volume of acid needed to neutralise an alkali." [4 marks]
→ Alkali in flask with indicator (1) + acid in burette (1) + add slowly until colour change (1) + record volume (1).
